01 — Project Overview

Context, Challenge
& Research Goals

Context

BBVA Perú sought to improve its mobile banking dashboard for high-net-worth individuals who frequently access banking in semi-public or sensitive environments.

Challenge

Users needed a way to control the visibility of financial products without compromising trust or creating cognitive friction in their daily banking flow.

Business Problem

Feature adoption was low despite clear user need. The mental models around hiding vs. deleting financial products were causing hesitation and abandonment.

My Role

Senior UX Researcher — led stakeholder alignment, research design, moderated usability sessions, synthesis, and strategic recommendations.

Research Goals

Understand privacy perceptions, map interaction expectations, and identify friction points in the visibility-toggle feature flow.

05 — Key Insights

What Users
Revealed

01
Users Expected Direct Interaction from Product Cards

"I would just tap the card itself — there must be something there. Why would I have to go somewhere else?"

Participants consistently attempted to long-press or swipe product cards directly, bypassing menu-based access points. The card itself was perceived as the natural locus of control.

UX Implication
Embed contextual actions directly within product card interactions rather than routing through secondary menus.
02
Privacy Was Interpreted as Security

"Hiding means it's protected, right? Like, no one can access it — not even if they steal my phone."

Users conflated visual privacy with cryptographic security. Many believed hiding a product also restricted unauthorized access, revealing a critical mental model gap that could undermine trust.

UX Implication
Clearly communicate what "hiding" does and does not do. Distinguish between visibility control and security protection.
03
The Eye Icon Was Universally Recognized

"The eye — that means show or hide. Everyone knows that. I've seen it everywhere, it's obvious."

Across all age groups and digital literacy levels, the eye icon was immediately understood as a visibility control. This was the feature's strongest affordance signal and should be amplified.

UX Implication
Center the eye icon as the primary visual metaphor for the feature — increase its prominence across all entry points and states.
04
Long Onboarding Text Was Ignored

"I don't read those explanations. If I need to read to understand, something is already wrong."

Modal copy explaining the feature was systematically skipped by all participant segments. Users relied entirely on iconography, visual cues, and button labels to form their understanding.

UX Implication
Replace explanatory text with progressive disclosure, visual demonstrations, and micro-copy embedded in the UI state itself.
05
Trust Depended on Biometric & Security Signals

"If it asks me for my fingerprint before hiding something, I know it's serious. That makes me feel safe."

Participants in older segments especially required authentication signals (fingerprint, PIN) to feel confident that a sensitive action was being properly executed and protected.

UX Implication
Offer optional biometric confirmation for the hide action, especially for older user segments, as a trust-building mechanism.

07 — Strategic Insights

What This Means
for the Product

01
Financial Privacy is Contextual

Users don't think about privacy in the abstract. They think about specific moments — a shared screen, a spouse nearby, a colleague on a video call. Design for those moments, not for a universal state.

02
Trust is Emotional

Functional correctness isn't enough. Users need to feel safe before they act. Every touchpoint in the visibility flow is an opportunity to build or break emotional confidence in the bank.

03
Control Drives Adoption

Users who felt a strong sense of agency over what they showed and hid were more likely to use the feature regularly. Perceived control is the primary adoption driver — not discoverability.

04
Security Perception Matters

The gap between what hiding does functionally and what users believe it does is a liability. Close this gap proactively or it will erode trust when users eventually discover the difference.

08 — Recommendations

Design Actions
to Take

Priority 1
Improve Discoverability

Surface the hide/show interaction directly on product cards via long-press or a contextual icon. Remove dependence on nested menus or settings pages.

Priority 2
Simplify Communication

Replace long modal copy with a 3-second animated visual demonstration of what hiding a product does. Show, don't tell.

Priority 3
Create Adaptive Experiences

Offer two interaction modes: a streamlined flow for younger users and a guided, confirmation-heavy flow for older segments. Let users set their preference.

Priority 4
Clarify Operational Consequences

Explicitly communicate that hiding is a display preference, not a security action. Use a persistent micro-label that differentiates visibility from access control.

"Research that began as a usability study became a strategic lens on how trust is built — and broken — in digital banking."

The findings reframed how the product team thought about the feature. Rather than optimizing a UI flow, they were now designing a trust experience. This shift influenced not just the visibility toggle, but the broader approach to sensitive interactions across the BBVA Perú mobile platform.

In financial experiences, users don't just need usability — they need to feel protected.
